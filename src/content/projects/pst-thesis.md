The state has always been in the business of knowing its citizens; computation just
made it embarrassingly good at the job. This was my thesis for the Political &amp;
Social Thought program at UVA — a hundred pages spanning political philosophy, public
policy, and science fiction, asking what computational technology does to the
relationship between a citizen and a state.

## What I did

The argument moves through three registers. First, the philosophical one: what
legibility means for a state, drawing on Scott's *Seeing Like a State* and the
long tradition of treating the census as an instrument of power rather than a
neutral count. Second, the policy one: how smart-city infrastructure, predictive
systems, and algorithmic benefits administration actually operate today, and where
their failure modes concentrate. Third, science fiction — not as decoration, but
because it is where the futures get argued about before they arrive.

The conclusion I landed on: the state is more involved in your life than ever, and
how you feel about that is, mercifully, still up to you. The technology settles
less than people expect. What it does is lower the cost of a kind of knowing that
was always wanted and previously unaffordable.

## Reflection

I finished this thinking the interesting problem was surveillance. I now think that
was the easy frame — the one that lets you feel clear-eyed without committing to
anything. The harder problem is administrative: not the state watching you, but the
state *deciding* about you, faster and more consistently than any human clerk could,
with no obvious place to stand and object.

Writing a hundred pages about it did not make me more certain. It made the question
more specific, which is probably what a thesis is for. Most of what I've worked on
since has been some version of the same question wearing different clothes, and I
didn't plan that.
