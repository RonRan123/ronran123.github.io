A summer as a Coding it Forward fellow, embedded with the City of Boston as a product
manager on the city's open data portal. Selected from 4,500+ applicants, at a 2%
acceptance rate, for a program that puts early-career technologists inside government.

## What I did

The portal serves 13,000+ monthly users — journalists, researchers, civic
developers, and a long tail of residents arriving with one specific question and no
patience for a taxonomy.

I ran the initial user and event exploration across that traffic: what people
searched for, where they gave up, which datasets absorbed most of the demand. The
finding was unglamorous and actionable — the most-requested datasets were buried
several clicks deep behind a category structure that made sense to the people who
maintained it and to nobody else. I used that to restructure the paths to the
highest-demand data and cut the average number of clicks to reach them.

## Reflection

This is the smallest project on this page and the one I think about most.

Open data is usually discussed as a transparency question — whether the data is
published. But publishing is the cheap part, and a city can be fully compliant with
every open data commitment while remaining functionally opaque, because the thing
you need is on page four behind a word you wouldn't have guessed. Access is an
interface problem long before it is a policy problem.

It also gave me a fairly unsentimental view of what constrains public-sector
technology. The people I worked with knew exactly what was wrong with the portal.
What they didn't have was an uninterrupted month to fix it. That is a different
problem from the one most civic-tech writing assumes.
