Forge is a student-run software consultancy at UVA. I was engineering director for
two years, which in practice meant three jobs: shipping the recruiting platform,
starting a consulting branch, and teaching people to write software.

## What I did

**The platform.** I designed and built the MVP of Forge's recruiting platform —
matching 750+ students against 120+ companies, and cutting time-to-match by 15%.
Most of the difficulty was not technical. Matching is easy to model and hard to make
people trust, and a student who doesn't trust the match ignores it and emails the
company directly, which defeats the point.

**The consulting branch.** I founded the branch that took on short- and
medium-term projects for clients around the University community. It grew
engineering revenue 22% and, more usefully, gave students real deadlines from people
who were not their professors.

**The course.** I taught 71 students software engineering through a 140-hour
curriculum built to get them ready for a nine-week internship — not to teach
computer science, which they were already getting, but the parts nobody teaches:
reading an unfamiliar codebase, asking a good question in a standup, shipping
something you're not proud of because it's Thursday.

## Reflection

The platform is the line that fits on a resume. The course is the thing I'd do again.

Building the MVP taught me that a matching product's hard problem is legitimacy, not
accuracy — a lesson that keeps recurring in work I do now on systems that decide
things about people. But the course is where I could actually see the effect. You
teach someone to read a codebase they didn't write and you've changed what jobs
they can take.

I also learned, running a volunteer engineering org, how much of leadership is just
maintaining the conditions under which other people can do good work, and how little
of it is doing the work yourself. I was slow to accept that.
