I spent a year of college reading about street-level bureaucracy and the year after
that building software that automates it. The overlap is larger than I expected.

Michael Lipsky's argument, roughly, is that policy is not made in legislatures. It is
made by the caseworker deciding which form counts as proof of residency, by the officer
deciding which stop is worth making. These people have discretion because the rules
cannot anticipate every case, and the aggregate of their small judgments *is* the policy
as citizens actually experience it. You cannot supervise your way out of this. There are
too many decisions and too few supervisors, and the decisions that matter most are the
ones the rulebook didn't foresee.

For most of the last century the response was proceduralism: write more rules, require
more documentation, make the caseworker show their work. It doesn't eliminate discretion
so much as relocate it — into which box gets checked, and which exception gets filed.

## The same shape, faster

Agentic systems reproduce this structure almost exactly, with two changes.

The first is volume. A caseworker makes a few dozen consequential judgments a day. An
agent makes thousands, and makes them consistently, which means an error in judgment is
no longer an error — it's a policy. The old system's saving grace was that its failures
were *uncorrelated*. One caseworker's bad day did not become every caseworker's bad day.

The second is that the explanation problem inverts. You could always ask a caseworker
why. The answer might be self-serving or reconstructed after the fact, but it was an
answer, and it was falsifiable — you could ask the next person in line and compare. An
agent's post-hoc rationale is fluent, plausible, and generated by a different process
than the one that produced the decision. It is not a worse explanation than the
caseworker's. It is a differently untrustworthy one, and our institutions have no
practice at this kind.

## What this rules out

The tempting move is to demand interpretability — make the system show its work, the way
we made the caseworker show theirs. I think this is the proceduralist error repeating
itself. Making a system explain its reasoning did not make bureaucratic discretion
accountable; it made it *documented*, which is a different and lesser thing. Documentation
is what you produce for an auditor. Accountability is what happens when someone can be
made to change.

The more useful question is the older one: what recourse does the person on the other
side of the decision have? Not whether they can see the reasoning, but whether they can
contest the outcome and reach someone with the authority to reverse it. Administrative
law figured out a long time ago that the right to appeal does more work than the right to
an explanation. We have spent an enormous amount of effort on the second and almost none
on the first.

This is the part I keep coming back to in my own work. It is easy to build an agent that
decides. It is considerably harder to build the thing underneath it that lets someone say
*no, look again* — and harder still to make that path fast enough that using it is
rational. When the appeal takes six weeks and the decision took four seconds, there is no
appeal. There is only a form.

I don't have a clean answer here. But I'm fairly confident the interesting work in the
next few years is in the recourse layer, not the explanation layer, and that we'll get
there late because explanation demos well and recourse doesn't.
