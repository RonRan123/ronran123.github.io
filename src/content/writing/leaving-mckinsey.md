**Summary**

- **What I came for and found:** I learned a lot at McKinsey from leaders who read my work, judged my thinking, and invested in me. But by design, that trust resets with every new study and every new team
- **What AI changes:** when AI makes polished pages routine, the content that analysts deliver no longer proves understanding. The people who would invest in you have a harder time telling who to invest in, and mentorship becomes harder
- **Why I'm leaving:** I want to also hone my judgment through results, not just by how people react to my work. Getting that kind of feedback at the AI frontier meant leaving

***

McKinsey was never part of the plan. In 2023, I was set on becoming a software engineer. McKinsey's application opened first, so I applied to sharpen my interviewing before engineering recruiting began. Then, unexpectedly, I got the offer.

Over the next few weeks, I messaged a lot of people. I looked for those who had a technical background, chose McKinsey, and later went back to tech. From those dozens of conversations, one in particular stuck with me. He had been promoted faster than his engineering peers, he said, because he was good at the things that engineers didn't want to do or could not do well.

That was what I was missing. I knew how to build things. But several projects I built stalled or had less impact than I wanted. I never learned how to make work stick through people. I wanted McKinsey to teach me the skills of judgment and influence, from leaders who had already mastered those skills. If it meant double the hours and half the pay, I was willing to take it.

## McKinsey at its best

I did gain those skills. I found studies and people that made the work exciting, regardless of how long it took. My favorite clients would often take a lot out of me. I would stay up till 4 AM perfecting the pricing strategy document, but I did it because I felt real pride over my work. I created the document from start to finish, presenting my work directly to the senior VP of the organization. I was accountable for his reaction to the content, good and bad. That work I did for him transformed into something more. The VP would reach out to me directly about questions he had. He would pull me into more strategic initiatives, from planning significant long-term capital investment projects to getting invited to meetings with the president of the organization.

Getting there took time. It took a second stint with that client before the team trusted me to lead the work from start to end. The trust came from doing the simple things right during the first stint: building an involved Excel model, creating succinct pages for other people's sections. I had to show I could learn quickly and deliver consistently before earning something bigger.

Along the way, I stumbled. There was a time when my email drafts were too long or when my pages were poorly formatted. Then another time when my Excel model linked the wrong data together, creating a skewed impact estimate. My stumbles grew more significant. The time I had to re-ghost a deck because the storyline wasn't persuasive. Or rework an analysis because I didn't anticipate an obvious client objection. Each time, the leadership team gave me direct feedback and showed me how to push my thinking further. Then they trusted me to fix it.

The apprenticeship I found was invaluable. Someone read my work, judged my thinking, and invested in me. I learned to anticipate the team's reaction before I shared anything. I learned to temper my habit of opening dozens of tabs and disappearing down rabbit holes, and to find what really mattered. I returned to that client because I had earned credibility, and I left with a confidence to handle difficult topics with the most senior clients.

## Falling apart and reflecting on the work

After that study, I felt like a baller. I had successfully pushed beyond my comfort zone, created real client trust, and worked on something exciting. I felt like I had figured out my BA toolkit and was well on my way to the next challenge.

The next study was the one I was looking for: a client and topic at the frontier of tech. I owned a workstream for one of the business units in this larger company strategy document. It was a real chance to figure out the core driving market trends and strategic opportunities available. Four weeks in, I was rolled off.

I stumbled again. Each iteration on the deliverables had something critical that I missed. I didn't structure the problem well enough or anticipate the next steps. I kept too many loops open and fell behind. Even though I was asking thoughtful questions in PS and building detailed evidence-backed pages, I was struggling. The team needed someone who could deliver more in a shorter period of time. On the pricing study, the leaders had watched me fix earlier mistakes so a stumble was a reason to coach me. This team had known me for four weeks. The only evidence they had of how I thought was my pages, and the pages showed gaps. Given the pace the client expected, rolling me off was a reasonable call.

When I heard the news, I first felt relief. Each day felt like I was digging myself deeper into the hole, where each mistake eroded the already diminishing trust I had with the team. The nights of 4.5 hours of sleep and long-distance travel had impacted me. The second thing I felt was shame. How had it all fallen apart so quickly? How did I go from the best study experience to then getting rolled off?

The EM and ED were generous in their feedback and time afterwards. I took their feedback to heart, seeking out due diligence (DD) opportunities to get more practice structuring the problem and acting quickly against a set of outputs. All that practice under pressure further solidified my toolkit. My skills got better, and I took them into every study after. But the trust I built did not travel with me. I had to earn it anew with each team.

Comparing these experiences, I came to realize there were a few conditions I needed to perform my best work. The best work had a combination of real ownership, a team invested in me long-term, and depth built from exposure to the problem for a long time. But by design, we transition and switch across so many studies that these conditions "reset" and have to be earned repeatedly with each new team.

Finding your people and the right studies is hard. Even when by some combination of hard work and luck I found it, that trust between the team and me had to be re-earned. Re-earning trust is the trade-off for broad exposure in a short time. It's how the firm has always worked. But AI started changing how analysts were evaluated.

## What AI changes for the analysts

Some think that AI will usher in a golden age for consulting and others think the end. For what it's worth, I largely agree with [Bob Sternfels](https://hbr.org/podcast/2026/01/where-mckinsey-and-consulting-go-from-here). Even as the work shifts toward outcome-based projects and more ambitious client questions, clients facing AI uncertainty will still need capable leaders to advise them on their hardest problems.

But that is a question I'm less interested in. There is a more immediate question about how AI is changing the experience for the talent at McKinsey. Talent that starts with the analyst class (BAs, Associates, Specialists). Analysts who choose to work long hours in exchange for client opportunities and to learn from firm leaders. Leaders who will teach you how to build your human skills (judgment, agency, vision, influence, etc.) while also giving specific, tailored feedback to your areas of growth.

AI strains this relationship.

As an analyst, gaining mentors and sponsors depends on your ability to demonstrate a strong work ethic and interest in the subject matter. Partners read your deliverables (pages, models), evaluate your thinking, and decide to invest in you. The work you produce signals the quality of thinking and depth of understanding. This is how I found caring leaders and created step-up opportunities. The manual work I did, while imperfect, showed my willingness to learn and put in the work. The grueling effort was rewarded in the form of real, caring relationships so that I could take on bigger tasks.

With superpowered tools, SKILL.md files, and pre-built firm AI products widely available, deliverables became easier to create. The deliverables became more routinized and commoditized. In the short term, an analyst may be able to dazzle by being a few months ahead of the curve and producing quality content quickly; but the gap between great and average tends to shrink with technology. In a study of 758 BCG consultants, [Harvard Business School researchers](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700) found that AI improved the work of bottom-half performers by 43%, compared with 17% for the top half. The average analyst became more "productive," creating more pages and building models faster.

When the work of the analyst was more manual, there was less "polish" in the first iterations, but there was also more room to iterate. The analyst would be given more guidance and direction that would shape their intuition for approaching the work. It was hard to create quality content without developing a clear understanding for the industry and problem first. AI made the "polish" on the day zero answers better, and those initial outputs became the default starting point for expectations rather than more ghosted or scribbled pages.

In recent client studies, instead of showing the old-school approach, managers tend to kick off by establishing the tools that new joiners should rely on. Tools ameliorate the pressure to deliver quickly by speeding up the content creation process, while previous methods slow down that process initially. When I asked a question in PS about how the partner thought about a certain topic, they would point out that an AI tool could produce a better answer than they could and suggest I spend some time there. Instead of team whiteboarding on the first couple days, I would be furiously prompting alone. Instead of the AP or EM delegating a certain task and showing how to approach it, they now had the tools to do it themselves quickly. Why invest that time in someone else when it could be done much faster yourself?

The partners and the managers were right to promote these AI tools, both for the team and themselves. But as "polished" pages became common among new analysts, it got harder to decide whom to mentor. While early investment in analysts diminished, the gap between expectations and reality widened. Assuming an EM produced a well-storylined document, it was easy to "fill" in the pages with AI-generated content and a surface-level analysis of the problem. Analysts had less reason to build a real understanding before creating pages, and leaders had less reason to teach them how. When studies reward speed, both sides skip the slow way.

I too fell into this trap. On one of my DDs, a new mode had come out, and I was able to create the first few pages I needed on the study within an hour. The content passed my EM's check, and we presented it as a sample to the partner in PS. The partner saw through the garbled mess I had made and pushed me to simplify the bullets on the spot. I stuttered and meandered. I didn't understand what I put on the page. In this new industry filled with daunting jargon, I had no inner model for how the pieces connected with one another. I had to redo the work, but fortunately I knew how to do it because I had practice with the "manual" method of building an understanding.

Some of this may be growing pains to a new model of work. However, my recovery depended on practice that newer analysts may find harder to get. AI relieves short-term study pressure while long-term skills stagnate. As expectations rise with each study, the work itself becomes less of a training ground for creating a strong foundation from which human skills can be built.

In this environment, the best analysts have to train themselves. They will seek out opportunities to learn AI skills outside their client work and deliberately push their own "manual" thinking. But if joining McKinsey was a bet on learning from leaders who mentor you, teaching yourself makes the long hours harder to justify.

## Chasing different feedback loops

For me, I did find my people. I did find those who care for me deeply. I was finding studies that gave me real step-up opportunities to be in the client room and present my work. Why not stick around with the people I found and build upon those opportunities?

Staying would give more opportunities where I'd develop my pattern recognition skills and train my judgment further. But I would be developing those against the reactions that others had for my work. I'd be judging the quality of my work by what my EM's review of the work was, by the semi-annual review feedback that partners would leave for me, and by the client's comments in the room. I'd never see the prices go live or see the details of the impact of any strategy document I made. I had moved on before it played out.

I grew worried that judging my work only by how others reacted, and not also by whether the recommendation held up, might open a gap between my understanding of an industry and the conviction I had for what mattered. A gap exacerbated by the time delay of being an advisor, waiting to hear secondhand how impactful the recommendations were. Staying one layer removed reduced my ability to experiment directly, waiting to see patterns form across clients rather than testing more boldly.

This trade-off is endemic to consulting, and for a century it worked. Rotating across clients built a breadth of experiences few operators could get. A client's approval was a reasonable proxy for results because the client knew their own organization and the firm knew what had worked across the industry. Both assumed the future would look like the past. But when new technology reshapes an industry in months instead of years, approval may no longer track what works. Keeping judgment sharp may increasingly depend on fresh results, in loops tight enough to keep pace with the change. The one signal that can't be faked is whether the recommendation actually worked.

McKinsey still produces more Fortune 500 CEOs than any other company. But if I [take the potential impact of AI disruption seriously](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3LZN2UUxDos&t=2330s), the advantage shifts. Judgment trained by advising many companies is likely to become less valuable than judgment formed inside one, in a faster feedback loop. As one [Fortune article](https://fortune.com/2025/09/25/mckinsey-ceo-pipeline-fortune-500-global-500-consulting-ai/) notes, tech firms "often elevate leaders who came up through engineering or product ranks and whose credibility comes from building and scaling platforms rather than analyzing them." Credibility that is earned from making and living with the consequences of a decision. If AI makes the cycle of learning faster, it becomes more important to ship a decision, watch what happens, and own what comes next directly. That is the credibility I want, earned by building and graded by the results.

## What's next and the people

Over the last few months, I've been reconnecting with that part of me that loved building new projects. I built [my own running app](/projects/vibe-coded-running-app) and used it every week. Somewhere between fixing what would break and adding what I wished it had, I remembered how much fun building is. I'm taking that into a new role at a [startup in SF](https://artificialanalysis.ai/), where I'll find out quickly whether what I build works.

I'm leaving with real gratitude. The people I found here taught me what I came for. More than once, someone put me in front of a senior client before I felt ready, and then helped me get ready. That's how the timid college graduate version of me learned to walk into unfamiliar rooms and contribute meaningfully. I saw the same growth and eagerness for reinvention in the people around me, in the AI workshops I led and in the new analysts I welcomed in their first week.

If you ever told me what my work was missing and then let me try again, thank you. If you were one of the analysts I leaned on during a hard study and became a friend along the way, thank you. You're the reason this was a hard place to leave.
